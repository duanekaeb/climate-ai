One brain for three ecobees

Your ecobees each see one floor and report one floor at a time. Climate AI treats the house as one connected system. It watches all nine temperature points (three thermostats and six room sensors), learns how the floors push heat into each other, and checks every change against the weather. Then it adjusts the schedules to cut total runtime without letting a room you're using get uncomfortable.

Your question 1

Machine learning, or Claude tweaking in real time?

Both, in layers. Models learn how your house behaves and make the real-time decisions through a plain controller. Claude, on your own subscription, verifies and tunes them a few times a day: nightly, weekly, and when something looks off. Claude stays out of the real-time loop, where it would cost far more and control no better. The models are good enough for daily decisions, not minute-by-minute prediction.

How the layers work
Your question 2

Does it put occupied rooms first?

Yes, with the hardware you have. 8 of your 11 rooms already report occupancy: six SmartSensors plus the Hallway and Bedroom thermostats. The Twins' Room, Olive's Room and Foyer have no sensor yet, so the app uses bedtimes there and shows their temperature as unknown. The app builds its own estimate for each room from them (live motion, sleep windows, learned habits) and never trades an occupied room's comfort for runtime. Most runtime savings come from empty floors, timing and the linked-floors rule.

Your question 3

How does it connect to the ecobees? Can we go direct?

Two links from your own server: your ecobee account sign-in for history and settings (ecobee stopped issuing developer keys in 2024), and HomeKit on your Wi-Fi for live readings within seconds. There's no deeper way in: no local API, the room sensors use a closed radio, and bypassing the thermostats means replacing them.

The write-up

How Climate AI runs your three units together

Yes, linking the floors makes sense. Letting each thermostat optimize only its own floor is a common way multi-zone houses waste runtime, because the floors end up working against each other.

What happens today

The main floor sees no motion, so ecobee's Smart Away lets the hallway float warm, to around 80°F (Phase 0 checks which Smart Away variant fired). You're upstairs, and that ecobee is holding 77°F. The main floor is now 3°F warmer than the upstairs. Warm air rises through the open stairwell, and the ceiling between the floors carries heat up as well.

The upstairs unit was already the hardest-working one, under a hot roof and probably with ducts in the attic. That extra load is the likely reason it hits 100% duty; the history study measures how much of it is the floor and how much is sun and heat. It runs all afternoon, the Girls' Room drifts, and the main floor's "savings" cost the house more than they saved.

ecobee can't see any of this. Each thermostat reports only itself, so its reports can't show one floor creating work for another.

Your instinct, with one refinement

Linking the floors is right. Keeping the main floor a little cooler than the upstairs, moving both floors together on setback, and bringing both back together on recovery should beat letting them drift independently.

The refinement is that the right offset changes with conditions. On a mild, cloudy day, a cooler main floor may only add main-floor runtime. On a 95°F sunny afternoon, keeping it 2°F cooler may save the upstairs far more than it costs downstairs. So the offset is something the app learns, separately for different outdoor temperatures, amounts of sun and times of day. Try the Floor coupling tab in the mockup to see the best offset move as the day gets hotter.

The rules the controller runs

Linked floors

When the main floor is empty but someone is upstairs, the main floor doesn't float. Its target is tied to the upstairs target plus a learned offset. The starting offset is 1°F cooler than upstairs.

Learns: offset by temperature, sun and hour

Setback and recovery move together

When the whole house is empty, both floors set back together with the main floor still the cooler one, as in your example: main 80°F, upstairs 82°F. The setback gap is learned separately from the 1°F occupied offset. On recovery the main floor starts first, so the upstairs cools down with less heat coming from below. Your rule (upstairs 82→78 means main goes to 77) is the starting point.

Learns: recovery lead, 0–45 min

Pre-cool with the forecast

On hot, sunny days the upstairs is cooled 1–2°F below target before noon. The outdoor air is cooler then and the AC removes heat more efficiently. The upstairs then coasts through the solar peak. On mild days the app skips it, because there it only adds runtime.

Learns: depth, start time, the forecast threshold

The right sensors at the right time

ecobee comfort settings decide which sensors count. Upstairs uses the Toy Room by day and the Girls' Room at night. Main floor uses the Living Room, Kitchen and School Room by day, and the Hallway thermostat at night as the nearest reading to the Twins' and Olive's rooms, which have no sensors. The wing uses the Office 9–5 and the Bedroom at night. The app writes these into each ecobee program and turns Follow Me off so ecobee doesn't override them. ecobee temperature holds use the Home setting's sensors, so the current time block's set is written into Home too.

Bed / Office runs on its own

Unless the data shows the main floor affecting the wing, it stays independent. Its savings come from its schedule, with the office empty in the evening and the bedroom only at night, and from sensor sets. If coupling shows up in the data, the wing joins the linked rules.

Protect the equipment and the air

Minimum run and off times prevent short cycling, and cycles per hour are tracked as a health signal. Indoor humidity has to stay under about 58%: a strategy that saves runtime by raising humidity loses. A hold someone sets by hand always wins until it ends or they hand it back, and the app records it as feedback. During a vacation or a utility energy-saving event the app stands aside.

What "best" means, written down

minimize Σ over units wu · runtimeu (wu = the unit's power draw; 1.0 each until measured) subject to occupied rooms inside their comfort band ≥ 97% of minutes every setpoint inside your hard limits ≤ 1 change per unit per 30 min, ≤ 2°F per change indoor humidity ≤ 58% no unit short-cycling (runs < 5 min)

Runtime is a fair start. Units differ in size, though, so a minute of a 3-ton main unit costs more than a minute of a 2-ton upstairs unit. A circuit-level power monitor (an Emporia Vue 3, or a Shelly EM Gen3 per unit) with a clamp on each condenser lets the app optimize energy instead of minutes.

Occupancy

Rooms in use come first

Yes, and your house already has what it needs. 8 of your 11 rooms report occupancy: six SmartSensors plus the Hallway and Bedroom thermostats. That's enough to run without buying anything; the three rooms without a sensor are covered below. The app keeps its own estimate for each room instead of trusting ecobee's flag. An occupied room's comfort is never traded for runtime. Expect room-by-room priority mostly to buy comfort; the runtime savings come from whole floors being empty, from timing, and from the linked-floors rule.

Why ecobee's own occupancy isn't enough

  • It's a 30-minute flag, not motion. ecobee's API reports a room as occupied if its sensor saw motion in the past 30 minutes, and the cloud copy updates about every 3 minutes. A room someone left 25 minutes ago still reads occupied.
  • Motion sensors miss still people. SmartSensors use PIR, which sees movement, not presence. Someone reading, working at a desk or sleeping goes undetected. Field studies of motion-based home control report exactly this: misses are worst at night.
  • A temperature hold switches sensor sets. ecobee's documented behavior is that a temperature hold uses the Home comfort setting's sensors, whatever the schedule says. An app that sets holds at night could quietly stop listening to the Girls' Room. The controller handles this explicitly (below), and it's a Phase 0 test on your firmware.

What the app uses instead

  • Live motion in seconds from each SmartSensor over the local HomeKit link, with a "seconds since last motion" reading. The app sets its own windows per room instead of ecobee's fixed 30 minutes.
  • Learned patterns per room, from the per-sensor occupancy history ecobee keeps in its 5-minute reports. The app learns when each sensor's room is usually in use (the School Room, Office, Living Room and so on), separately for school days, weekends and holidays.
  • Sleep windows for the Girls' Room and Bedroom. They count as occupied all night without needing motion, because motion sensors can't see sleepers.
  • Optional presence sensors (mmWave radar) for rooms where people sit still for long stretches, such as the Office during work hours. The SmartSensors are enough to start; add radar only if a room keeps getting marked empty while someone is in it. Door contacts on bedrooms help too: a door that closed after motion means someone is probably still inside.
  • Phones only for whole-house "adults away". The kids don't carry phones, so phones alone never declare the house empty.

Rooms without a sensor: Twins' Room, Olive's Room, Foyer

How the app treats them today

  • Temperature: unknown. They show as "no sensor" and are never given the thermostat's number as if it were theirs.
  • Occupancy: from schedules. You set bedtimes for the Twins' and Olive's rooms, and they count as asleep through the night, the same as the Girls' Room. The Foyer is a walk-through space with no comfort target.
  • Control at night: the main floor's night sensor set is the Hallway thermostat, the nearest reading to those two bedrooms. If they run warmer or cooler than the hallway, the app can't see it until they're measured.
  • The Foyer's unit: to confirm it's on the bedroom system, run only the bedroom unit for a few minutes and feel whether the Foyer vent blows.

How to learn them, cheapest first

  • Calibration week, no purchase. Move one of your SmartSensors into each room for about a week (the School Room's during a school break, say). The app learns how that room runs against the Hallway reading by hour, outdoor temperature and sun. After you move it back, it shows a labeled estimated temperature with a range.
  • A SmartSensor in each bedroom (about $50 each). Measures the room, adds occupancy, and can join the main floor's night set so the thermostat itself listens to the bedrooms. The best fit for rooms people sleep in.
  • A Zigbee temperature sensor (about $16). Only the app can read it, the ecobee can't use it, and it has no occupancy. Fine for the Foyer.

How occupancy steers each thermostat

An ecobee only averages whichever sensors are in the active comfort setting. It can't weight rooms. So the app works in two loops:

  • Slow loop, a few times a day: the sensor set for each time block (Toy Room by day, Girls' Room at night, and so on) is written into the comfort settings, including Home, because temperature holds use Home's sensors.
  • Every 3 minutes: the app learns how far each room usually runs from the thermostat's average, and picks the setpoint that keeps every occupied room inside its band. When two occupied rooms on the same thermostat disagree, the priority room for that time wins: upstairs, the Girls' Room at night and the Toy Room by day; in the wing, the Bedroom at night and the Office during work hours; on the main floor, the School Room on school days and the Living Room in the evening.

Occupancy and the linked floors

  • Main floor empty during the day, anyone upstairs: the main floor becomes a buffer tied to the upstairs target. It never floats to its Away setting. At night the main floor isn't empty: the Twins' and Olive's rooms are asleep, so both floors hold sleep comfort, and the offset only decides how cooling is shared between them.
  • Whole house empty: adults' phones away and no motion anywhere for 45 minutes and outside sleep windows. Both floors set back together, with the main floor still the cooler one.
  • Coming home: recovery is timed from the learned arrival pattern, with the main floor leading.
  • Bed / Office wing: sets back only when both rooms are empty.
i

What to expect from occupancy

Within one floor, shifting priority between rooms mostly buys comfort, such as not over-cooling an empty sunny room to fix a shaded one. The runtime savings come from whole floors being empty and from the linked-floors rule. Studies of multi-zone buildings (university residence halls, and zone-level control tests in larger buildings) find that setbacks save less than predicted, because an empty zone pulls heat from its neighbors. That's the same effect you see between your floors, and why savings are always measured on the whole house.

Machine learning

How it learns, adjusts, and dials in

The loop below repeats for as long as the app runs. Each pass narrows down what works in your house, and each change is tested in the real house before it's adopted.

1

Backfill, then watch

On day one, pull the 5-minute history ecobee still holds (about a year, possibly more; Phase 0 measures it) and the matching hourly weather from Open-Meteo's archive. Then study the afternoons your main floor already floated warm. You start with last summer as a baseline instead of waiting weeks. Nothing changes in the house yet.

week 1
2

Fit the weather baseline

For each unit, find the outdoor temperature where cooling starts (its balance point), then fit runtime against degree-days (how far, and for how long, the outdoor temperature sat above that balance point), sun and humidity. The model must predict held-out days well, with low scatter (CV(RMSE) ≤ 20%) and low bias, before any savings number is shown.

weeks 1–3
3

Fit the house's thermal model

A small physics model with learned numbers: how fast each zone gains heat from outside, how strongly the zones are coupled, what the sun adds, and how much heat each unit removes per minute. It's refit every night from the 5-minute data. It starts simple, and extra detail (an attic state, say) stays only if it predicts better. It runs in shadow, and its plans drive the house only once it beats the linked-floors rule in backtests (Phase 5).

weeks 3–6, in shadow
4

Simulate tomorrow

Each night, simulate candidate plans (offsets, pre-cool, recovery lead) against tomorrow's forecast, re-run them at 6 AM, noon and 3 PM or when the forecast shifts, and keep the cheapest plan that meets comfort. This is model predictive control in its simplest form.

nightly
5

Test it in the real house

The simulation's favorite becomes a challenger. Days are randomly assigned to the current champion or the challenger, and the comparison is weather-normalized on total-house runtime. The success measure and 2–3 checkpoints are fixed before the test starts, and the early checkpoints use stricter (wider) intervals so the extra looks together still keep about a 10% false-win rate. Peeking daily and stopping on a good-looking day would manufacture false wins.

1–3 weeks per test
6

Adopt, then keep tuning

The winner becomes the champion. Settings become condition-specific (one offset on 95°F sunny days, another on 82°F cloudy ones), and Bayesian optimization, run mostly against the simulator, picks the next values to try. When a model drifts, the app looks for a cause: season, a dirty filter, low refrigerant.

Cup·dTup/dt = (Tout−Tup)/Rup + (Tmain−Tup)/Rmu + ks·max(0, Tmain−Tup) + aup·Sun − Qup·onup(t) + gup Cmain·dTmain/dt = (Tout−Tmain)/Rm + (Tup−Tmain)/Rmu + (Tbed−Tmain)/Rmb + am·Sun − Qmain·onmain(t) + gmain Cbed·dTbed/dt = (Tout−Tbed)/Rb + (Tmain−Tbed)/Rmb + ab·Sun − Qbed·onbed(t) + gbed

Reading the model

  • C: how much heat a zone can soak up (furniture, drywall, air).
  • R: resistance to heat flow. A small Rmu means a strong main-to-upstairs link; a large Rmb confirms the wing is independent.
  • ks: the one-way stairwell term. Warm air moves up more easily than down.
  • a·Sun: solar gain, using sun aimed at your roof and windows.
  • Q·on(t): heat removed while a unit runs, taken from ecobee's runtime seconds.
  • g: people, cooking and electronics, partly predicted from occupancy.

This is a standard grey-box RC model of the kind used in building research, and it can be fitted from thermostat and room-sensor data. A Kalman filter keeps it current between refits.

What the math does

  • Fits baselines and the house model (statsmodels, scipy)
  • Runs the 3-minute control loop and enforces your limits
  • Simulates plans against the forecast
  • Computes savings, intervals and experiment results

Deterministic, testable and fast. It never needs a language model to keep the house comfortable.

What Claude does

  • Reads the results each night and spots what's unusual
  • Explains it in plain English, with the weather separated out
  • Proposes the next experiment and argues for it
  • Answers your questions with real queries, not guesses

Claude works on a slower cycle (nightly, weekly, or when something unusual happens). It never sits in the real-time loop, and everything it proposes goes through the same limits as the controller.

When is it "dialed in"?

Large effects, like stopping the main floor from floating to 80°F, can usually be confirmed within a season: a 10–15% change in total runtime needs roughly 1–7 weeks of test days per option (about 2–14 weeks in all), depending on how noisy the house's daily runtime turns out to be. Small refinements (1° vs 2°, ten minutes of recovery lead) are a different story. Confirming a 5% difference in one house can take 50–200 days per option, so those are judged mainly in the simulator and adopted when the simulator and a real test point the same way. In practice, "dialed in" means experiments stop finding differences larger than their own uncertainty. Then the app shifts from testing to watching for drift. Heating season starts a new round, because heat rising from the main floor then helps the upstairs.

How Claude pushes the models toward optimal

What slows learning in a house isn't computing. It's weather noise: a small difference between two strategies takes days of data to show through day-to-day variation. So Claude can't make the house dial in faster than the weather allows. What it adds is making each day of data count: choosing what to test, catching mistakes early, and explaining results. It does that in six ways:

Mine the history first

The backfill contains every afternoon your main floor already floated warm. Claude has the models treat those afternoons as natural experiments, tests the house already ran on its own (this is the history study), with two checks against fooling itself: the bed wing should show no effect, and the same analysis run on similar afternoons when nothing floated should find none either. Those checks matter because sun, weaker AC on hot afternoons and Smart Away all peak at the same time, so a simple before/after comparison would overstate how much the main floor heats the upstairs. The result is a starting estimate, confirmed later with small planned tests.

Kill bad ideas in simulation

Each candidate runs against real past weather in the house model first. Only candidates that look better there, by more than the model's own uncertainty, get a real day in your house.

Size every test before it starts

Claude runs the sample-size math first. A single house can usually confirm a 10–15% change within a season (roughly 2–14 weeks). Confirming a 5% change can take 50–200 days per option, so small tweaks get judged mostly in simulation.

Stick to the plan, check honestly

Each test fixes its success measure (total-house runtime, never one unit) and 2–3 checkpoints in advance. Checking every day and stopping at the first good-looking result would produce false wins, so early checkpoints use stricter intervals. Neither Claude nor the optimizer can redefine success once data arrives.

Audit the next-test choice

The optimizer's math picks the next setting to try. Claude reviews the choice, explains it, and can veto it with a reason, for example "a heat wave starts Thursday; test pre-cooling then."

Fix the model, not just the settings

When prediction errors show a pattern, such as always underestimating the upstairs on west-sun afternoons, Claude proposes a new feature. The backtest and shadow days decide whether it's adopted.

How robust the models are, honestly

A public set of about 60,000 house models fitted to ecobee thermostat data gives a realistic benchmark. Their median indoor-temperature error is about 0.6°F one hour ahead and about 2.5°F a day ahead, and roughly 1 in 10 fit poorly. Your house has three coupled zones measured through compressor runtime alone, so expect somewhat worse.

That's good enough for coarse decisions: which offset band, whether to pre-cool, how early to start recovery. It isn't precise enough to trust a minute-by-minute trajectory, which is why the 3-minute rules keep correcting course. Without a power monitor, the model learns heat flows in runtime minutes rather than watts.

Build order follows from that. Version 1 is the weather baselines, the history study and the linked-floors rule, checked against the weather baseline and then tuned with one planned switchback. The full house model and automatic tuning join only when they beat that rule in backtests.

Fallbacks

  1. A model fails its checks → the last good model takes over
  2. No good model → the controller runs the simple linked-floors rules
  3. Claude unavailable (plan limit reached, sign-in expired) → changes waiting for sign-off stay pending, the house runs on the current models, and reports catch up later
  4. Server or controller down → holds expire within 2 hours and each ecobee runs its own schedule

Can Claude change the model code itself?

Settings, yes, through the checks. Inside the app, Claude proposes features and parameters through tools, and the checks run on their own before anything goes live.

Code, only as a pull request. In Claude Code on this repo, Claude can write a new model as a pull request. The scoring code and the most recent weeks of test data (held back so a model can't be tuned to them) stay read-only and outside its reach: a 2026 study found AI coding agents tried to tamper with unlocked evaluations in about half of runs. CI runs the backtest and you merge. A merged model still starts with shadow days.

How weather gets separated from strategy

An expected runtime for every day

A baseline model per unit predicts runtime from that day's weather. The balance point is searched from 30–90°F (the CalTRACK method), and degree-days are computed from hourly temperatures, not the daily average, because the relationship isn't linear.

Savings = expected − actual

Run the baseline with this period's weather and subtract what the units actually ran. This is the IPMVP "avoided energy" method. Each unit's runtime is metered separately, which makes the estimate tighter than a whole-house electric meter would.

A waterfall every week

The latest period is compared with the one before it (the mockup uses the last 14 days vs the 14 before), and the change is split into weather, sun, the strategy, and noise. That's the "What changed the total" chart in the mockup's Did it work? tab.

Randomized switchbacks for small effects

Before/after comparisons are fine for big changes. For tuning, days are randomly assigned to one strategy or the other, so weather averages out across both. A UC Berkeley (Center for the Built Environment) study that re-analyzed 639 commercial buildings with a simulated intervention found randomized designs more accurate and faster than pre/post comparisons, though even there a result took about 36 weeks. The first few hours after each switch are dropped to account for thermal mass.

Claude Agent SDK

Running Claude on your subscription

Claude Opus 5.5 runs through the Claude Agent SDK on your home server, signed in with your own Claude plan. No API key and no per-token bill. It wakes a few times a day to verify and tune the models; everything that has to run continuously runs without it.

Setup, once

Sign the agent in with your plan

  1. Run claude setup-token on any computer and approve it in the browser. It prints a token that lasts one year.
  2. Put it in the agent service's environment as CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN.
  3. Make sure ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is not set anywhere on the server. If it is, it silently takes priority and bills the API.
  4. Never run the agent in "bare" mode. Anthropic recommends it for scripts, but it ignores subscription sign-in. Pin the SDK version, and the app checks at startup that it's signed in to your plan.
Staying inside your plan

A small, predictable footprint

  • About one run a night, one a week, and at most three triggered runs a day (anomalies close together are merged into one run). Questions you ask come on top.
  • Runs happen at night, when you're not using Claude yourself. Your plan's session, weekly and Opus limits are shared with your own Claude use.
  • Tools return summaries, not raw rows, so each run reads little. Turns are capped.
  • If a limit is hit, the run stops with a reset time, the app reschedules it after the reset, and the morning digest says it was late. If only the Opus limit is hit, the app can rerun on Sonnet 5.5 instead of waiting.
  • The token expires after a year. The app warns you 30 days ahead, and immediately if sign-in ever fails.

import os
from claude_agent_sdk import query, ClaudeAgentOptions, ResultMessage, ResultError, tool, create_sdk_mcp_server
from climate import store, scheduler
from climate.control import guardrails

assert "ANTHROPIC_API_KEY" not in os.environ, "would bypass the subscription and bill the API"

@tool("query_runtime", "Runtime by unit vs weather-expected, summarized",
      {"start": str, "end": str, "resolution": str})
async def query_runtime(args):
    summary = await store.runtime_summary(args["start"], args["end"], args["resolution"])
    return {"content": [{"type": "text", "text": summary}]}   # small: keeps plan usage low

@tool("sign_off_change", "Approve or hold a change the models queued",
      {"change_id": str, "decision": str, "reason": str})
async def sign_off_change(args):
    problem = guardrails.check_sign_off(args["change_id"], args["decision"])  # must have passed the automatic checks
    if problem:
        return {"content": [{"type": "text", "text": f"Rejected: {problem}"}], "is_error": True}
    await store.record_sign_off(**args, source="claude-nightly")
    return {"content": [{"type": "text", "text": "Recorded"}]}

house = create_sdk_mcp_server(name="house", tools=[query_runtime, sign_off_change])  # + the other tools

options = ClaudeAgentOptions(
    model="claude-opus-5-5",
    fallback_model="claude-sonnet-5-5",       # used if Opus is overloaded
    effort="medium",                          # "high" for the weekly report
    system_prompt=open("agent/prompts/nightly.md").read(),
    mcp_servers={"house": house},
    allowed_tools=["mcp__house__query_runtime", "mcp__house__sign_off_change"],
    # dontAsk still runs tools that never prompt (file reads, Agent), so remove built-ins outright
    disallowed_tools=["Bash", "Read", "Write", "Edit", "Glob", "Grep", "Agent",
                      "NotebookEdit", "WebFetch", "WebSearch"],
    permission_mode="dontAsk",
    cwd="/srv/climate/agent-empty",             # an empty directory: no secrets within reach
    max_turns=30,
)

async def nightly():
    try:
        async for msg in query(prompt="Verify yesterday. Separate weather from strategy. Review the "
                                     "pending changes. Write the morning digest.", options=options):
            if isinstance(msg, ResultMessage):
                if msg.terminal_reason == "completed":
                    await store.save_run(msg.result, msg.session_id)
                else:                               # a failed request can still say subtype "success"
                    await store.flag_failed_run(msg.session_id, msg.terminal_reason, None)
    except ResultError as e:                 # turn cap, plan usage limit, sign-in failure
        await store.flag_failed_run(e.session_id, e.terminal_reason, e.api_error_status)
        if e.api_error_status == 429:            # usage limit: try again after the reset
            await scheduler.retry_after_reset("nightly")
        # pending changes simply wait; the house runs on the current models

ecobee connection

How the app talks to your ecobees and sensors

The app uses two links at once, both straight from your server, with no Home Assistant or Apple in the path. The only direct link to the hardware that exists is HomeKit on your local network. Everything else that sounds more "direct" either doesn't exist or would mean replacing the thermostats.

Link 1 · required

ecobee cloud API, signed in with your ecobee account

The only source for compressor run time per 5 minutes, per-room history, about a year of backfill, and the comfort settings and sensor sets.

  • Sign-in uses the python-ecobee-api library (the library behind Home Assistant's ecobee integration, which added account sign-in in 2026.3) for login, MFA and token refresh only. The app makes every data call itself.
  • Every 3 minutes: thermostatSummary (what changed, which units are running). On change: thermostat with sensors, events, program and the last few 5-minute run-time readings (about 15 minutes behind).
  • Hourly plus nightly: runtimeReport for the official 5-minute record (lags up to an hour).
  • About 30,000 requests a month in total, well under the 85,000 a month ecobee set for developer-key apps. Nothing is published for the account sign-in route, so the app stays inside that budget anyway.
Link 2 · strongly recommended

HomeKit, locally, with your server as the controller

The Python library aiohomekit (version 4.0 or later) lets your server pair with each ecobee over your Wi-Fi.

  • Pushed within seconds: each SmartSensor's temperature, motion and occupancy; each thermostat's temperature, humidity, and whether it's cooling or heating.
  • Polled: seconds since each room's last motion, and which equipment is running.
  • Keeps working when ecobee's cloud or the sign-in route breaks, including basic timed holds.
  • Pair directly rather than through Home Assistant: Home Assistant hides the ecobee-specific fields this app needs, and its mode switch creates permanent holds.
  • A thermostat pairs with one HomeKit controller, so the ecobees leave Apple Home. If the family uses Siri for the thermostats, that's the trade-off to decide.

Pairing HomeKit, step by step

No Apple device, Apple Home, Homebridge or Home Assistant. The home server becomes each thermostat's HomeKit controller, and each SmartSensor shows up as an extra device behind the thermostat it's linked to. Three pairings in all: Hallway, Toy Room and Bedroom.

  1. Network. Run the server on Linux, on the same network (LAN/VLAN) as the thermostats, because discovery uses mDNS. Give each thermostat a fixed address (a DHCP reservation).
  2. Free each thermostat from Apple Home. A thermostat pairs with one controller at a time. On the thermostat, choose "Disconnect from HomeKit" or "Reset HomeKit" (the menu path varies by model; confirm it on the device). Anyone using Siri for the thermostats loses that.
  3. Start the HomeKit service (docker compose --profile homekit up -d). The app's Setup page lists thermostats that are ready to pair.
  4. Pair in Setup. Pick the unit and press Pair. The thermostat shows a fresh 8-digit code; type it in (XXX-XX-XXX). The pairing keys are saved, encrypted, in the database before anything else happens.
  5. Check the sensors. They map to rooms by name. Newer SmartSensors report occupancy and motion and push them within seconds. ecobee's "seconds since last motion" counters can't push, so they're read every minute.

Checked against the aiohomekit 4.0.1 source and its test accessory, not yet on a real ecobee. Two traps the app avoids: the library's aiohomekitctl pair command reports success but loses the keys (the thermostat then needs a HomeKit reset), and the Home/Sleep/Away setpoint fields permanently edit the schedule, so the app never writes them. Full steps: docs/HOMEKIT.md.

Rules the ecobee adapter follows

Writes

  • Only short timed holds (holdType: holdHours, 1–2 h), renewed while healthy. Never indefinite holds.
  • Every write is read back. The sign-in library silently swallows HTTP errors.
  • Program edits (sensor sets) start from a fresh read and check the program hasn't changed since. Otherwise an edit you made in the ecobee app gets overwritten.
  • HomeKit holds: write the end time first, then the hold, then verify. Never touch the Home/Sleep/Away setpoint fields, which permanently edit the schedule.
  • Temperatures are rounded before converting to tenths of °F.

Sign-in and reliability

  • The account-login route broke or changed about three times in 14 months. Use TOTP or SMS MFA on the ecobee account; push and email MFA don't work.
  • Store only the newest refresh token, encrypted. Don't keep the password. A re-sign-in screen handles MFA codes.
  • The web client ID is a setting, not hard-coded, because ecobee can change it.
  • The library logs access tokens at debug level, so that logger stays quiet.
  • A circuit breaker switches the controller to the HomeKit link when the cloud fails.

Roadmap

Build order

Each phase ends in something you can use. Apart from Phase 0's HomeKit pairing and a one-night test hold, nothing writes to a thermostat until phase 4, and complexity is earned: the simple linked-floors rule goes first, and the full house model only replaces it if it does better. It's October, so the backfill hands you last summer's cooling data right away. Heating season gets its own learning pass, and that's when heat rising from the main floor starts working in your favor.

  • Confirm all six SmartSensors show up on both links and which unit feeds the Foyer; order the two stairwell sensors; database schema and Open-Meteo for your location

Done whenall 9 temperature points stream live and the Phase 0 tests have answers

History and the live dashboard

Done whendaily runtime per unit matches ecobee's own reports within 1%

  • Room occupancy with three states (occupied, asleep, empty), learned patterns, and bedtimes for the rooms without sensors; optional calibration week for the Twins' and Olive's rooms

Done whenbaselines pass their checks on held-out days, and you've spot-checked a week of room states

Phase 3weeks 3–5

Claude as analyst (read-only)

Controller, Suggest mode, Linked floors

Done whenupstairs maxed-out minutes are near zero and total-house savings have a 90% interval

Phase 5week 8 on

House model, tuning, Auto mode, heating season

  • The full house model, forecast pre-cooling and automatic tuning, each adopted only if it beats the linked-floors rule in backtests
  • Predicted arrivals, equipment health alerts, a heating-season learning pass

Done whenexperiments stop finding gains larger than their uncertainty
